How to write an executive resume

An executive resume has one job: to show a hiring leader, in under a minute, that you can deliver results at scale. Here is how to build one.

By the Virtue Careers team. Updated November 6, 2026. 4 minute read.

An executive resume has one job: to show a hiring leader, in under a minute, that you can deliver results at scale. It is not a list of everything you have done. It is a short, focused case for why you are the right person for the next role.

What makes an executive resume different

A mid-level resume often lists tasks. An executive resume shows scope (how big your responsibility was) and results (what changed because of you). Hiring boards and senior recruiters want to see three things quickly:

  • The size of what you have led: people, budget, revenue, regions.
  • The outcomes you created: growth, savings, turnarounds, launches.
  • How you lead: building teams, making decisions, working with a board or senior leaders.

Start with a clear summary

Under your name and contact details, write a short summary of three to five lines. Say who you are, the scale you work at, your strongest proof and the kind of role you want next. Skip empty phrases like "results-driven leader". Use facts instead.

Example (numbers are made up for illustration)Operations executive with 15 years leading teams of 200 or more across manufacturing and logistics. Cut costs by $12M and raised on-time delivery from 91% to 98%. Seeking a Chief Operating Officer role in a growth-stage company.

Show scope first, then results

Under each role, add one line that sets the scene: team size, budget, revenue or number of countries. Then write three to five bullet points. Each bullet should follow a simple pattern: what you did, and the result it produced, with a number where you can.

Example bullet (made up for illustration)Rebuilt the regional sales structure, which lifted revenue from $38M to $52M in two years while keeping the team the same size.

If you do not have exact figures, use honest estimates and ranges, such as "about 30%" or "more than 100 people". Never invent numbers. A hiring manager will ask about them in the interview.

How long should it be

Two pages is the usual length for an executive resume. A third page can be fine for very senior leaders with a long and relevant career. Focus most of the space on the last 10 to 15 years. Older roles can sit in a single short line each, with just the title, company and years.

Pass applicant tracking systems

Many companies use applicant tracking systems (ATS) to store and search resumes before a person reads them. A simple layout is read more reliably than a complicated one. A few safe habits:

  • Use one column and standard headings such as Summary, Experience and Education.
  • Avoid tables, text boxes, charts and images, especially in the header.
  • Use the words from the job description where they are true for you, in natural sentences. Do not stuff in keywords.
  • Send the file type the employer asks for. If they do not say, a PDF or a Word file are both common.

What to include and what to leave out

Worth including

  • Board seats, advisory roles and committee work.
  • Speaking, publications and awards that relate to the role you want.
  • Certifications and degrees, kept brief.
  • A link to your LinkedIn profile, with a custom web address.

Safe to leave out

  • A photo, unless it is standard where you apply (see our resume vs CV guide).
  • Age, marital status and other personal details.
  • An "objective" statement. Your summary replaces it.
  • "References available on request." Employers know to ask.
  • Outdated skills and very old roles that do not help your case.

Common mistakes

  • Listing duties instead of results. "Managed a team" tells the reader nothing. "Led 40 people and cut turnover by a third" does.
  • Making it too long. Three dense pages are rarely read in full.
  • Sending the same resume for every role. Adjust the summary and top bullets so they match the job.
  • Inconsistent formatting. Mixed fonts, dates and bullet styles look careless at executive level.
  • Typos. Read it aloud, then ask someone else to read it.

Checklist before you send it

  1. Does the top third of page one show who you are and your strongest proof?
  2. Does every role show scope and at least one result?
  3. Is it two pages (or three at most) with a clean, simple layout?
  4. Does it match the job you are applying for?
  5. Do your LinkedIn profile and resume tell the same story? Our LinkedIn guide for executives shows how.
